Materials, at their essence, are networks defined by homogeneity: uniform bonds, fixed
thicknesses and discrete length scales. Mechanical metamaterials, while representing
structurally more diverse microstructures, remain defined by the homogeneity of
their unit cells, pore sizes or repeating features1,2. In contrast, as network science has
revealed, real-world and biological systems—from the Internet to the brain—derive
their function from broad, multiscale variability in connectivity and link length3–6.
Here we introduce scale-rich metamaterials, a design framework that embeds network
heterogeneity into mechanical metamaterials, achieving orders-of-magnitude
heterogeneity in ligament lengths, thicknesses and connectivity. Governed by only
two parameters, scale-rich networks span orders of magnitude in structural features,
overcoming previous constraints in metamaterial design. Translating these network
models into physically realizable materials, we use simulations and experiments to
show that scale-rich metamaterials exhibit properties inaccessible to traditional
single-scale systems, including highly tunable elastic anisotropy, delocalized nonlinear
deformation with high energy absorption, enhanced energy dissipation under
dynamic loading and programmable elastic-wave control. This network-based
framework establishes a minimal and universal model for engineering multifunctional
materials whose mechanical behaviour emerges directly from scale diversity, and also
opens avenues of enquiry for network science itself.

At their core, materials are networks: in crystalline solids, atoms form
regular lattices defined by uniform bonds, and in mechanical metamaterials, a distinct graph encodes the interactions among structural
components. These graphs share a critical feature—an exceptional
homogeneity of their local properties. Crystalline lattices are defined
by unique bond lengths and interaction strengths, and even disordered
systems such as amorphous microstructures show narrow distributions of connectivity and bond strength. Mechanical metamaterials, or
architected materials, inherit this homogeneity: despite their structural
diversity, they are typically governed by one or a few discrete length
scales7, even in the presence of hierarchy2,8 or disorder9. Unit-cell design
further enforces uniform connectivity10 and geometric compatibility
constraints11,12, while plate- and shell-based architectures also rely on
fixed thicknesses and uniform pore sizes13,14.
However, as network science has revealed in the past two decades,
real-world networks rarely exhibit the homogeneity of crystalline or
amorphous solids. Instead, systems as diverse as protein–protein interaction networks, social networks and global infrastructures such as the
Internet show remarkable structural variability: node degrees span
orders of magnitude and interaction strengths cover broad ranges3–6.
This heterogeneity underlies their robustness15–17 and resilience18, facilitates dynamical processes19, and enables control through a small subset
of nodes20. Even physical networks21–23, such as neuronal and vascular
networks, whose nodes and links have material presence, are highly
heterogeneous, with degrees, link lengths and strengths spanning
several orders of magnitude24.

Elements of multiscale organization are also a hallmark of biological
systems, defining their mechanical and transport properties. Dragonfly
wings, for example, display vein lengths spanning more than three
orders of magnitude, from ℓmin = 0.1 mm to ℓmax ≈ 50 mm (ref. 25). Leaves
exhibit equally complex venation patterns, with length scales ranging from ℓ ≈ 10−1 mm to ℓ ≈ 103 mm and connectivity spanning roughly
3 decades26. Spider webs are made of string whose lengths extend from
ℓ ≈ 1 mm to ℓ ≈ 103 mm (ref. 27), and bones represent hierarchical architectures built from rod- and plate-like elements, whose lengths range
from ℓ ≈ 100 μm to ℓ ≈ 1,500 μm and thicknesses from λ ≈ 40 μm to
λ ≈ 400 μm (ref. 28).
Over the past two decades, network models have provided a powerful language for describing systems whose structure spans many
scales29–32. Graph-theoretic descriptions increasingly link such structural organization to mechanics in natural and engineered materials,
collagen and other biopolymers33, paper34, self-assembled nanoparticles35, and composite fibre networks36. These studies show that disorder
and network topology can be used to tune mechanical response37, for
example, through the selective pruning of elastic networks38. Yet no
generative framework exists for architected materials in which length,
thickness and connectivity simultaneously span orders of magnitude,
nor one predicting how this structural heterogeneity shapes mechanical performance.
To address this gap, here we introduce a design paradigm for
mechanical metamaterials that leverages a network-science-based
approach to generate architectures with a controlled, yet broadly

We refer to these materials as scale-rich (SR) networks, as they allow
the simultaneous coexistence of lengths, thicknesses and connectivities that span orders of magnitude within the same system. Using
both computational modelling and experiments, we show that SR
metamaterials, governed by only two parameters, enable properties
unattainable in single-scale systems: a 190-fold tunable range of elastic
anisotropy across densities, stable and delocalized nonlinear deformation with high energy absorption, enhanced energy dissipation under
dynamic loading, and programmable elastic-wave responses. Finally,
we derive an analytical model that connects the total stored energy to
geometrical features such as ligament length, thickness and degree,
unveiling the mechanical role of the multiple length scales present in
the system. Taken together, these results establish SR materials as a
versatile platform for engineering multifunctional systems with programmable, multiscale performance. They also provide a minimal yet
powerful framework for inverse design and open avenues of enquiry
for network science.

The SR model
To construct an SR metamaterial, we start with a unit square domain
(L = 1) and iteratively add ligaments whose thickness λ decreases with
time t as λt = λ0t−α, where λ0 is the initial thickness and α is the decay
exponent. The generation process consists of two steps (Fig. 1a):
(1) choose a random nucleation point (xt, yt) within the L2 domain and
a random angle θt; (2) grow a ligament of thickness λt from the nucleation point in both directions, defined by θt, until it intersects other
ligaments or the boundary. We repeat steps 1 and 2 until a predefined
time t = T or until we are unable to add new ligaments, leading to a
state of jamming22,39 (see Supplementary Information, section 1.5 for
more examples and Supplementary Information, section 1.8 for the
implementation). Each new ligament stops at its first intersection,
being fused together, resulting in a planar network without overlaps.

Both regular and irregular architectures have unique characteristic
length scales, determined by either a unique or narrowly varying coordination number (degree, k), length (ℓ) and ligament thickness (λ)14,42.
The distinguishing feature of an SR network is its lack of characteristic
scale for any of these three defining dimensions. Indeed, as the ligament
thickness follows λt = λ0t−α, the overall thickness distribution P(λ) follows
the power-law distribution P(λ) ≈ λ−(1+1/α) (Fig. 1e and Supplementary
Information, section 1.2).
To determine the ligament length distribution, we must consider
that the ligament lengths decay as ℓt = ℓ0t−β, where β = 0.5, except at the
boundary, where β = α (Supplementary Information, section 1.1.1). As
we derive in Supplementary Information, section 1.3, the resulting
ligament lengths follow an Erlang distribution: for high ℓ (ℓ ≈ 2 ) the

A fundamental network characteristic is the degree (or coordination
number) distribution3,4,29. It is tempting to consider each ligament
intersection a node and each connecting segment a link, an approach
often adopted in physical networks43,44. However, in the SR model, ligament thickness spans multiple orders of magnitude, prompting us to
adopt a representation frequently used in transportation networks23,45,
where each ligament (or road) is conceptualized as a single node, and
intersections are represented as edges (Fig. 1b). In this representation, the degree distribution P(k) follows the power law P(k) ≈ k−γ with
γ = 3 (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Information,
section 1.4). The power law emerges owing to a spatial preferential
attachment29: longer ligaments have linearly more opportunities to
intersect with newly added ligaments (Supplementary Fig. 11).
Taken together, we find that in SR networks, all three salient features
of the system’s architecture—coordination number (degree, k), length
(ℓ) and ligament thickness (λ)—follow power-law distributions. In other
words, whereas in conventional material systems k, ℓ and λ are constant
or vary in a very narrow range, in SR materials they span several orders of
magnitude within the same sample, encoding the coexistence of many
different length scales. The unique heterogeneity of the SR network
raises an important question of whether SR metamaterials possess
physical properties distinct from those of single-scale natural and
architected materials.

SR material microstructures
We begin by determining the mechanical characteristics of SR microstructures, comparing them against three single-scale microstructures
frequently used in engineering applications: the square lattice, the
hexagonal lattice and the irregular Voronoi tessellation. By generating
finite domains of each microstructure with a sufficiently large number of characteristic features, we confirm that the P(λ), P(ℓ) and P(k)
for square and hexagonal lattices result in delta functions, whereas
the Voronoi tessellation is characterized by narrow distributions
(Fig. 1i–k).
To extend the comparison beyond theoretical differences between
architectures, we experimentally and numerically determined the
mechanical properties of these metamaterial microstructures, using
vat photopolymerization 3D-printing (Methods) to fabricate
centimetre-scale specimens out of elastic–plastic polymer resin (Fig. 1d,
left), with a feature resolution of approximately 100 μm. We focused
on SR systems with T = 500—a point where the homogenized mechanical properties show convergence and boundary effects are minimized
(Supplementary Information, section 2.3). Given this feature resolution
and the 500-ligament constraint, the relative densities achievable with
vat photopolymerization 3D-printing ranged between 20% ≤ ρ ≤ 75%
(Supplementary Information, section 2.5); we can also demonstrate
lower fill fractions within the tunable-density phase ( ρ < 20%; Fig. 1c)
through the use of two-photon lithography (Fig. 1d, right) with submicrometre minimum feature sizes. Next, we characterized the linear
and nonlinear mechanical responses in the quasi-static and dynamic
regimes of these SR microstructures.

modulus E ≡ Ed /Es , where Es is Young’s modulus of the constituent
material (Methods).

While the hexagonal and Voronoi structures show a near-constant E
in all orientations (that is, an isotropic stiffness response, as expected46;
Fig. 2a(i),(ii)), the square lattice is highly anisotropic, displaying high
stiffness parallel to its struts and minimal stiffness in any other direction (Fig. 2a(iii)). In contrast to these near extremes, we find the behaviour of the SR structures to be modulated by α: for α ≈ 0, the stiffness
is nearly isotropic (Fig. 2a(iv)); and as α → 1, a principal orientation of
maximum stiffness emerges (Fig. 2a(v),(vi)). Altogether, the full set of
SR architectures exhibited highly tunable (an)isotropy exceeding that
of the three conventional geometries combined.
To quantify the mechanical implications of this observed range in
anisotropy across the full space of SR architectures, we plot the elastic
anisotropy ratio ηE ≡ Emin /Emax ∈ (0, 1] , where Emin ≡ min(E ) and
Emax ≡ max(E ), as a function of relative density for the reference geometries and 2,600 distinct SR microstructures with different α and λ0
(Fig. 2b). We confirm that the hexagonal lattice (0.87 ≤ ηE ≤ 0.94) and
the Voronoi tessellation (0.88 ≤ ηE ≤ 0.97) remain nearly isotropic across
a wide range of relative densities. For the square lattice, the anisotropy
decreases from ηE = 0.03 at ρ = 20% to ηE = 0.55 at ρ = 70%. By contrast,
we find that the SR materials cover 77% of the available area on the
density–anisotropy plot (Fig. 2b), reaching values not accessible to
the reference systems. Indeed, by changing α and λ0, we can attain
highly anisotropic systems (ηE ≤ 0.2) across a wide range of densities
(17% ≤ ρ ≤ 64% ) as well as nearly isotropic systems (ηE ≥ 0.85) in the
range 33% ≤ ρ ≤ 72%. Simultaneously, we can generate systems of widely
varying anisotropies at any fixed relative density: at ρ = 20%, we span
0.04 ≤ ηE ≤ 0.78; at ρ = 40%, we span 0.12 ≤ ηE ≤ 0.88; and at ρ = 70%,
we span 0.26 ≤ ηE ≤ 0.93. Directed growth approaches (Supplementary
Information, section 2.15) further extend the achievable coverage to
more than 96% of the density–anisotropy space.
To elucidate the origin of this tunable anisotropy, we plot the massnormalized (that is, specific) minimum and maximum stiffness values
(Emin /ρ , Emax /ρ ) as a function of the decay parameter α across the entire
sample set (Fig. 2c). Although the minimum specific stiffness is largely
independent of α, increasing α while keeping density constant enables
an increase in the maximum achievable specific stiffness, leading to
robust tunability in the architecture space. This tunable anisotropy is
best captured by the (α, λ0) configuration space (Fig. 2d), which shows
that anisotropy increases with α and decreases with λ0. To validate our
finite-element model predictions and confirm the isotropic and anisotropic responses, we performed uniaxial compression experiments
across various SR geometries and orientations, confirming that at
constant density increasing α directly leads to greater anisotropy
(Fig. 2a(vii)–(ix), Fig. 2b, filled black circles, and Supplementary Information, section 2.7).

Delocalized nonlinear deformations
Next, we characterized the nonlinear response of SR metamaterials by
conducting large-deformation uniaxial compression experiments
(Methods) on the 3 reference geometries and SR systems of α = 0, chosen to minimize the impact of elastic anisotropy and emphasize the
effects of the heterogeneity of ligament lengths (Fig. 3a; 3 specimens
each, all at nominal ρ = 42%; experimental results for SR systems with
α > 0 are discussed in Supplementary Information, section 2.11.).
Experiments on the square, hexagonal and Voronoi tessellations
showed qualitative differences between each architecture (for example, in the densification strain); repeated samples of each architecture
showed minimal variation (Supplementary Information, section 2.8).
By contrast, all realizations of SR architectures (Fig. 3a, orange dotted
lines) showed a monotonically increasing stress–strain response, coexisting with a wide envelope of responses, elucidating the tunability
enabled by the SR framework and breaking the structure–response
constraints observed in the single-scale reference geometries.

measured as the integral under the stress–strain curve to an engineering strain of 40%—before the onset of complete densification. These
toughness metrics, when plotted against relative density (Fig. 3b),
showcase the ability of SR architectures to match or surpass the
mechanical resilience of the three reference architectures.

Efficient dynamic impact absorption
Looking beyond the quasi-static regime, an ideal impact-absorbing
material maximizes the energy dissipated during the impact event
while minimizing the peak force49. We performed impact experiments
on SR and reference architectures at relative density ρ = 40%, using a
rigid impactor released with controlled initial energy corresponding
to an instantaneous strain rate of ε· = 100 s−1 at impact.
Using a high-speed camera, we analysed frames during the impact
event (Fig. 4a), finding that similar to the quasi-static experiments,
the square lattice fails in a layer-by-layer fashion with fractured ligaments localized within sequentially forming horizontal crushing bands.
The square lattice showed a sharp initial peak force that dropped to
nearly zero followed by subsequent peaks corresponding to local

compactions (Fig. 4b, blue curve). The hexagonal lattice also exhibited extreme localization owing to the fracture of diagonally oriented
planes in as little as 2 ms post-impact. Although the Voronoi tessellation diffused damage more effectively, it also showed clear localized
crush bands, leading to a high maximum force (Fig. 4b, purple curve).
The SR geometry, by contrast, lacks not only well-defined crushing
bands but also diffused damage through the structure, leading to
the most stable force–time behaviour (Fig. 4b, orange curve), with
low initial peak force and a lower compaction force than the Voronoi
geometry.
We extracted the overall peak force, Fmax, and the change in kinetic
energy of the impactor before and after the event, representing the
energy dissipated by the architecture, allowing us to measure the specific energy dissipation as

Steering elastic waves using SR materials
SR metamaterials also support programmable elastic-wave propagation, owing to their widely tunable elastic anisotropy, fully parameterized with only two parameters (α, λ0). To quantify direction-dependent
wave propagation in SR metamaterials, we performed Bloch wave
analysis on SR microstructures across the (α, λ0) design space—here
reinterpreted as representative volume elements in an infinite tiling
(Fig. 5a and Supplementary Information, section 2.16). Leveraging the
tunability of SR metamaterials, we designed acoustic metamaterials
out of quasi-isotropic SR architectures. Using the effective refractive
index concept from optics neff = c0/vs, which compares the wave velocity in the constituent material c0 to the velocity vs of a wave travelling
through an SR metamaterial made of the same constituent material,
we find that for SR metamaterials, neff spans 1.5 ≤ neff ≤ 8.3 in the (α, λ0)
configuration space, demonstrating a substantial range in achievable
refractive indices compared with the hexagonal and square lattices,
which span 1.0 ≤ neff ≤ 1.9 within the same relative-density limits
(20% ≤ ρ ≤ 75%; Supplementary Information, section 2.17).
The refractive index tunability of SR microstructures enables multiple elastodynamic functionalities such as wave guiding or lensing, with
applications in subwavelength imaging and energy harvesting50,51. We
designed a Luneburg-like cylindrical lens of diameter d in a pixelated
gradient-index (GRIN) approach52 (Fig. 5b and in Methods), and validated it via steady-state dynamic finite-element method, focusing elastic perturbations from an incident plane wave (Fig. 5c). Displacement
fields reveal that waves traversing the lens focus on the opposite side,
producing semi-circular wave fronts at the focal point and indicating
that SR lenses qualitatively follow Luneburg patterns50. The displacement intensity (squared displacement magnitude) contour along the
vertical line through the focal point shows a peak that is an order of
magnitude larger than the side peaks, indicating energy concentration into a region approximately 0.1d wide (Fig. 5d). For comparison,
a randomized reference lens constructed by randomly selecting SR
cells at each radius produced a focal intensity roughly three orders of
magnitude weaker, with a broader peak and side lobes of comparable
magnitude, confirming that focusing arises from the GRIN arrangement
of the SR pixels. Spanning such a breadth of neff enables the generation of graded materials with smoothly changing acoustic properties

Beyond these properties, SR metamaterials offer broad design
flexibility (Fig. 6a) through independent control of orientation,
seed density and dimensionality, enabling tailored heterogeneity
that expands beyond unit-cell constraints—from filling arbitrary
non-convex domains (Fig. 6b) to selectively nucleating ligaments
in a manner that further minimizes localization (Supplementary
Information, section 2.12) or produces extreme anisotropy (Supplementary Information, section 2.9). As a demonstration, we programmed the ligament orientation (θi = f(xi, yi)) and, inspired by the
microstructure of Citrus maxima56, the radial seed-density gradient ((xi, yi) ~ ri, where ri is the radial distance of ligament i from the
centre) of an SR system to divert load-transfer pathways around
a fragile poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) payload (Fig. 6c,d).
Photoelastic imaging shows stress fringes nucleating within
the payload for square and hexagonal lattices of equal density,
whereas the SR system suppresses them (Fig. 6e(i)–(iii)); nonlinear
finite-element simulations confirm that at identical macroscopic
strain, the SR-protected payload stress is reduced by up to 4.9-fold
(Fig. 6e(iv)–(vii)).
The sequential generation strategy of the SR system extends naturally to three dimensions by iteratively adding non-crossing plates or
beams (Fig. 6a(v),(vi), yielding an analytically defined phase diagram,
power-law area and degree distributions, and the same anisotropy
trends as in two dimensions—anisotropy increasing with α (Supplementary Information, sections 1.6, 1.7 and 2.18), broadening the SR
framework towards lightweight, robust and directionally tunable 3D
metamaterials.
Finally, SR materials open opportunities for network science by
revealing features inaccessible to networks without a material instantiation. Here link length and thickness—undefined or inconsequential
in abstract networks—are fundamental, giving rise to phenomena, from
anisotropy to stress propagation, beyond the reach of traditional network models. Taken together, these results suggest that architected
network materials could open a rich domain of enquiry for network
science.

Methods
Elastic homogenization
We evaluated the directional stiffness of the SR and reference geometries in the linear-elastic regime by applying computational homogenization using the finite-element method implemented with the
commercial software package ABAQUS/Standard. Owing to the aperiodicity of SR geometries, classical homogenization methods relying
on periodic boundary conditions do not apply. Previous approaches to
extracting effective elastic properties of aperiodic materials include
prescribing a displacement-controlled uniaxial strain to estimate
Young’s modulus along a given direction, provided the representative
volume is sufficiently large57, or applying a set of mutually orthogonal
affine boundary conditions to estimate the full stiffness tensor C, which
provides an approximation of effective stiffness58. Assuming that the
base material is isotropic and linear elastic (with material parameters
Es and νs), we adopted the latter approach to characterize the complete elastic response of the SR system. Samples were discretized using
six-node, quadratic plane-strain continuum elements (CPE6) with a
mesh size obtained via a mesh convergence study (Supplementary
Information, section 2.4). For SR geometries, meshing was performed
using an adaptive strategy where the seed size for each strut was computed based on its width. Thus, the total number of degrees of freedom
and the minimum element size varied across geometries.
Sample fabrication
Samples were fabricated using vat photopolymerization using a desktop 3D-printer (Phrozen Sonic Mini 8K). Geometries generated using
the model were converted into three-dimensional solid bodies by adding a prescribed, uniform thickness that yielded a plane-strain response
(that is, avoiding out-of-plane deformation). To aid in experimental
repeatability, the specimens were printed with monolithic top and bottom plates with a thickness equal to 5% of the height of the architecture.
For the nonlinear and impact experiments, we used a commercially
available resin (Anycubic Tough Resin 2.0) with a high elongation to
break (for representative stress–strain curves of monolithic specimens,
see Supplementary Information, section 2.1). For the experiments
within the linear regime, we selected a stiffer but more brittle resin
(Formlabs Clear v4) so that linear-elastic properties could be more
readily extracted. In each case, we printed using the manufacturer’s
recommended settings for exposure parameters at a layer height of
0.025 mm. After printing, samples were washed in isopropanol and
dried in air. The dried samples were then kept in storage away from
ambient ultraviolet exposure and tested 72 hours after printing.
Uniaxial compression experiments
Quasi-static compression experiments were performed on an Instron
5500 series load frame under displacement control. The lower compression platen was held fixed and the upper platen was given a prescribed
downwards velocity corresponding to a (quasi-static) strain rate of
5 × 10−3 s−1. To perform the areal Jacobian calculations, greyscale photographs were taken at regular intervals during loading. These photographs were thresholded to separate the printed beams from void
spaces. A custom image tracking script using the Python library OpenCV
was used to track the areas of each void as deformation progressed.
Drop weight impact experiments were conducted using an Instron
Dynatup drop tower. We used an impactor mass of 6.2 kg and a constant
initial height, set to 0.8020 m above the sample, to ensure a velocity
at impact of approximately 4 m s−1 (equivalently, an instantaneous
strain rate of 100 s−1 at impact). We used a high-speed camera to record
snapshots at 9,000 Hz during the experiment.

Nonlinear finite-element simulations
To numerically evaluate the nonlinear behaviour of the architecturepayload system in Fig. 6, we used the finite-element method implemented with the commercial software package ABAQUS/Explicit. As
in the elastic case, both the architecture and payload domains were
discretized using modified six-node, quadratic plane-strain elements
(CPE6M) with the same mesh density in the architecture. The architecture was modelled as elastic–plastic with hardening and the material
properties were taken from experimental data (Supplementary Information, section 2.1). The rigid payload was modelled as isotropic and
linear elastic with an elastic modulus 106 times that of the architecture.
The compression platens were modelled as analytical rigid surfaces,
with the bottom surface encastred and the top surface given a prescribed downwards velocity consistent with the experimental strain
rate ε· = 5 × 10−3 s−1 . We used the built-in general contact algorithm
between all surfaces (including self-contact within the architecture),
with a friction coefficient of 0.2 defining the tangential behaviour
and a ‘hard’ pressure-overclosure condition defining the normal
behaviour.
